How NeuroDetect works, in plain words

No medical or computer background needed. Each part below explains one idea, with a small drawing.

Why is this needed?

57 millionpeople lived with dementia in 2021; Alzheimer's causes 60–70% of cases and over 60% of patients live in low- and middle-income countries. WHO fact sheet
Up to 90%of dementia cases go undiagnosed in low- and middle-income countries (75% worldwide), often because specialist tests are not available. World Alzheimer Report 2021
About 42%of people with Alzheimer's also have depressive symptoms, so the two conditions overlap. Chi et al., Current Alzheimer Research, 2015
Why telling them apart matters: in a review of 284 patients whose depression looked like dementia ("pseudodementia"), 53% no longer met dementia criteria at follow-up, because their memory problems came from treatable depression (Connors & Brodaty, Psychological Medicine). Mistaking one for the other means either missing treatable depression or missing dementia.

How NeuroDetect helps: EEG machines are already common and far cheaper than brain scans such as PET. NeuroDetect uses one ordinary resting EEG to screen for Alzheimer's-type slowing and to check a depression marker at the same time, so a clinic without a scanner can decide who needs a specialist and who should also be screened for depression.

What is an EEG?

Your brain cells talk using tiny electrical signals. An EEG (electroencephalogram) picks these up with small metal sensors resting on the scalp, like a microphone listening to the brain's "hum". It is painless and takes about 10 to 20 minutes with the eyes closed.

Hospitals usually use 19 sensors. NeuroDetect only needs 5 of them, which makes a quicker, simpler setup possible.

Sign 1: the brain's rhythm slows down

When a healthy person rests with closed eyes, the back of the brain hums at about 10 waves per second (the alpha rhythm). In Alzheimer's, damaged brain cells make this rhythm drift slower, towards 4 to 8 waves per second (the theta rhythm).

NeuroDetect measures the slowing ratio: how much slow theta there is compared with alpha. A higher ratio means more slowing.

Sign 2: brain areas fall out of step

Healthy brain areas work like a team, rising and falling together. Alzheimer's weakens the connections between them, so the front and the back of the brain stay in step less often. Doctors call this a "disconnection".

NeuroDetect measures teamwork (scientists call it coherence) between the top-front sensor and each back sensor, on a scale from 0 (no link) to 1 (perfectly in step).{% if a %} In our data, healthy brains scored about {{ '%.2f' | format(a.centroids.healthy[2]) }} and Alzheimer's brains about {{ '%.2f' | format(a.centroids.alzheimers[2]) }}.{% endif %}

How the computer decides: voting slices

The recording is cut into 10-second slices. Slices spoiled by a blink or a head movement are thrown away. Each remaining slice is compared with two "typical" brains learned from real patients, a typical healthy brain and a typical Alzheimer's brain, and votes for the one it is closer to.

The result is the share of votes, for example "70 of 84 slices looked like Alzheimer's". One bad slice can't swing the whole result, and you can always see which measurement pushed the decision.

The mood check

Depression in older people can cause poor memory and concentration that look like dementia (sometimes called "pseudodementia"), but it is treatable. Some research suggests that in depression the left front of the brain is less active than the right. NeuroDetect measures this left-right balance with the F3 and F4 sensors.

What it can't do

  • It does not diagnose. {% if a %}About {{ 10 - (a.optimised.summary.sensitivity[0] * 10) | round | int }} in 10{% else %}Some{% endif %} people with Alzheimer's are missed, so a "no pattern" result does not rule it out.
  • It was trained on recordings from one hospital; other machines may behave differently.
  • The mood check is only a hint; a questionnaire is needed.

Technical summary for examiners
  • Channels Fz, O1, O2, F3, F4; 0.5–45 Hz band-pass; resampled to 250 Hz; 10 s non-overlapping epochs; adaptive peak-to-peak rejection (3× channel median, 500 µV ceiling, flat-channel check).
  • Alzheimer's features: log relative theta/alpha power at O1/O2 and theta-band magnitude-squared coherence Fz–O1/Fz–O2, selected from 35 combinations by nested subject-level CV.
  • Depression feature: frontal alpha asymmetry ln(F4α) − ln(F3α); a 70-combination search did not beat it.
  • Classifier: StandardScaler + nearest-centroid with cosine distance (chosen from six distance measures), applied per epoch; result = share of epoch votes. Depression model: Euclidean nearest centroid on one feature.
  • Data: OpenNeuro ds004504 (Alzheimer's vs healthy) and ds003478 (BDI-labelled depression). Validation: 10× repeated stratified 5-fold, subject-level, nested.
